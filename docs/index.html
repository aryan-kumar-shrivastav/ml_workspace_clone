Title & Abstract

Which pages need refreshing — and which just need watching?

Question: Given a page's search-performance signals and content attributes, can a model outperform a deterministic CTR-gap rule at identifying which pages are declining and deserve editorial attention? Data: 30,000 anonymised content pages from the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse, spanning 32 clients, with trailing-90-day search and engagement metrics. Method: Binary classification (trend_direction == "down") evaluated as a ranking problem, with a client-holdout split to prevent client-level leakage; Random Forest compared against Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, and a deterministic baseline rule. Result: Random Forest reached Precision@50 = 0.68 (68% of the top-50 flagged pages genuinely declining) vs the baseline's 0.24 — a 2.8× improvement at the most decision-relevant cut. Use: The ranked action queue (refresh / refresh-and-review-CTR / expand / monitor) is designed as decision support for a content editor's weekly triage pass — directional, not causal.
Introduction

The triage problem every content team has

A content team might manage hundreds or thousands of published pages, but they have time to meaningfully refresh only a handful each week. The question isn't "what drives rankings" in the abstract — it's a prioritisation problem: given everything we can see about a page today, where should an editor look first?

A common approach is a simple rule: flag pages that rank in the top 20 but have below-average CTR for their position tier. That rule is fast, explainable, and already useful. The question this capstone asks is whether a model trained on the same signals — plus content-structure and engagement features — can meaningfully improve on it at the top of the queue, where editor time is actually spent.

The output is a ranked refresh queue with reason codes and a confidence tier per page, designed to sit alongside a human review process rather than replace it.

Feature groups used:

  • Search signals: impressions, clicks, CTR, average position, days with impressions/sessions — all trailing 90 days
  • Content structure: word count, character count, content age, days since last update
  • Engagement: scroll rate, engagement rate, AI traffic percentage
  • Categorical tiers: position tier, impression tier, age tier, freshness tier, content type, intent, competition level

Excluded: client names, domains, URLs, raw query strings, any PII, and the label-source columns (trend_direction, trend_pct) from the feature matrix.

Filter applied: pages with zero impressions in 90 days and pages under 90 days old were removed before modeling (not enough signal to assess).

Methodology

Label definition, split design, and leakage checks

Label

is_declining_label = 1 when trend_direction == "down". This is an observed direction label — it states what was measured, not what caused it. Pages with a "down" trend in the 90-day window are the positive class.

Split design — client holdout

The split is grouped by client_id: 80% of clients (26) go to train, 20% (6) go to test. No page belonging to a test client appears in training. This prevents the model from learning client-level patterns and then being evaluated on the same clients — which would inflate scores and misrepresent generalisation to new clients.

Why not a random row split? Pages from the same client share writing style, content mix, and market segment. A random split leaks those patterns across the boundary — the model sees partial data from every client in both train and test, making the evaluation optimistic by design.

Leakage check

trend_direction and trend_pct — the direct sources of the label — are explicitly excluded from MODEL_NUMERIC_FEATURES and MODEL_CATEGORICAL_FEATURES in scripts/ml_utils.py. Verified at runtime in the w05 notebook: confirmed absent from the 52-feature matrix.

Baseline

A deterministic rule that scores pages by CTR gap: pages ranking ≤ 20 that fall below their position tier's mean CTR are flagged. Score = weighted sum of: CTR gap (30%), staleness (25%), trend direction (20%), thin content (15%), position quality (10%). No model training, fully auditable.

Models compared

  • Logistic Regression — linear baseline; tests whether a simple weighted sum of features is sufficient
  • Decision Tree (depth 5) — interpretable non-linear model; can print every decision path
  • Random Forest (200 trees, depth 10) — primary pick; captures feature interactions the rule and single-tree can't

Primary metric: Precision@50 — of the top 50 pages the model flags, how many are genuinely declining? This matches how editors use the queue: they work through the top of the list, so false positives at the top are the expensive failure mode.

Limitations & Honest Framing

What this work cannot claim

Observed, not causal. The model identifies pages associated with declining trend signals — it does not prove that refreshing them will improve rankings. Correlation in trailing metrics does not imply that any specific intervention causes the observed trend.
Small client holdout. Six held-out clients is a small test population. The directional result (model >> baseline) is credible; the precise figures (0.68 P@50) should be read as a directional estimate, not a stable measurement. Different 6-client samples would produce different numbers.
Near-invisible pages are a blind spot. Pages with fewer than 5 impressions in 90 days and no GSC position data are systematically missed — the model can only detect decline on pages with a measurable search footprint. Low-visibility pages need a different signal source.
Decision support, not decisions. The ranked queue is a starting point for a human review pass. An editor should confirm the reason codes make sense for each page before acting. The model flags candidates; the editor decides.
Starter dataset. Results are from the bundled anonymised starter dataset (30,000 pages). The full gated warehouse may produce different feature distributions and model behaviour.
